\begin{abstract}
We investigate whether a resource-constrained (49K-parameter, single-GPU) flow-matching policy can
replace hand-designed execution-control rules for contact-robust descent in robotic grasping. Through
six sequential, empirically-verified diagnostic fixes -- addressing run-to-run reproducibility, a
rejected over-regularization hypothesis, receding-horizon control instability, a training-distribution
conditioning gap, and a second training-distribution gap in sub-segment coverage -- we raise success
rate from 0\% to a held-out-validated 50\%. This remains below our own existing interpolation-based
baseline (75\%), and we report that honestly rather than obscure it. We isolate the primary remaining
bottleneck to approach-angle data sparsity in the training distribution -- necessary but not
sufficient to explain failure on its own, since a nontrivial fraction of failures still occur inside
the dense arc. Diagnosing this further, we find that $\sim$12.5\% of single-run trial classifications
disagree across otherwise-identical repeats: a genuine evaluation-protocol reliability limit for this
class of system, not an artifact of any one trial, which we report as a methodological finding in its
own right and which motivates repeat-based, majority-vote evaluation as standard practice rather than
trusting any single run.
\end{abstract}
